HbA1c: understanding your result

A plain-language guide to glycated haemoglobin and what the result means.

Published: 14 October 2026

Terms in this article (4)
  • HbA1c: glycated haemoglobin — HbA1c forms when glucose binds to haemoglobin in red blood cells and roughly reflects glucose exposure over the previous 2–3 months.
  • CGM – continuous glucose monitoring — CGM uses a sensor to repeatedly estimate glucose in interstitial fluid, rather than directly in blood.
  • TIR – time in range — TIR is the percentage of time sensor glucose stays within a defined target range.
  • GMI – glucose management indicator — GMI is a statistical estimate calculated from mean CGM glucose.
Educational content only; it does not replace individual advice from a healthcare professional.

What is HbA1c and why measure it?

HbA1c forms when glucose binds to haemoglobin in red blood cells. It roughly reflects glucose exposure over the past 2–3 months, with more recent weeks contributing more. It is not a current glucose reading.

Understanding mmol/mol and %

Laboratories may use mmol/mol (IFCC) or percentages (NGSP/DCCT). 0%. Always check the units.

HbA1c is not a meter reading

A glucose meter measures a specific moment; glucose changes with meals, exercise, stress and illness. HbA1c summarizes a longer period.

How CGM, TIR and GMI relate

CGM tracks glucose across day and night. TIR is the proportion of time in an individualized target range. GMI is a sensor-based estimate and may differ from laboratory HbA1c.

When HbA1c may mislead

Some anaemias, blood loss, transfusions, kidney disease and haemoglobin variants may distort the result. Discuss discrepancies with a clinician.

Use in diagnosis

Is one target right for everyone?

No. Below 53 mmol/mol (7%) may suit many adults, but goals depend on individual circumstances, including hypoglycaemia risk.

Practical example

8 mmol/L and HbA1c of 64 mmol/mol are not necessarily contradictory: one is a momentary reading, the other reflects a longer period.

Key message

HbA1c is useful but needs context. Do not change medication or insulin based on one number alone.
