Diabetic foot problems: why a small wound matters

Nerve damage and reduced circulation can turn a minor injury into a serious problem. Early care helps protect your feet.

Published: 13 November 2026

Educational content only; it does not replace individual advice from a healthcare professional.

What the term means

Diabetic foot problems include ulcers, infections and damage to deeper tissues associated with diabetes. They are not inevitable. Your personal risk varies, and regular foot assessments help identify changes before they become harder to treat.

Why an injury may not hurt

Neuropathy means nerve damage. Reduced sensation can hide pressure, pain or heat, so a blister or a stone in a shoe may go unnoticed. Reduced arterial blood flow can also delay healing. A painless foot is therefore not proof of a healthy foot.

What a professional checks

A foot assessment looks at skin, foot shape, footwear, sensation and circulation. Findings guide follow-up and specialist care. Ask about your risk level and your next assessment; a previous ulcer or amputation calls for particular attention.

If you find a wound

Contact your diabetes team, primary care clinician or specialist foot service today for a new ulcer, blister or injury. Protect it with a clean non-adherent dressing and reduce pressure; do not walk it off or cut away hard skin. A professional decides on offloading, dressings and any treatment for infection.

When to act immediately

A wound with fever, rapidly spreading redness, black tissue or a suddenly cold pale foot needs immediate emergency assessment. Call your local emergency number (112 in the EU) for confusion, collapse or rapid general deterioration. A hot swollen foot needs urgent assessment even without pain or an open wound.
