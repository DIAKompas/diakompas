No added sugar: what does the label really tell you?

A “no added sugar” claim can help when choosing food. On its own, it does not tell you how much carbohydrate you will eat. Check the nutrition declaration and your portion size as well.

What “no added sugar” means

In the European Union, this claim may be used only if a product contains no added mono- or disaccharides, or any other food used for its sweetening properties. If sugars are naturally present, the label must say so. Naturally occurring does not mean that the product contains no sugar.

5 g of sugars per 100 g or 100 ml. Neither claim describes all the carbohydrate or the energy content of the food.

Why carbohydrate matters too

The American Diabetes Association (ADA) explains that foods labelled “sugar-free” or “no sugar added” are not necessarily carbohydrate-free or lower in carbohydrate than the original version.

Carbohydrates are a group of nutrients that includes sugars and starch. Starch is found, for example, in grains and foods made from flour. Blood glucose means the concentration of glucose, or blood sugar, in the blood. Carbohydrate can affect it.

On an EU label, “of which sugars” is part of “carbohydrate”. Do not add these two figures together. Fibre is declared separately; the US “total carbohydrate” figure discussed by ADA includes fibre. US labelling instructions therefore cannot be applied unchanged to an EU food label.

A worked example: the portion matters

These figures are a fictional teaching example, not an analysis of a particular product. The example food contains 60 g carbohydrate, including 5 g sugars, per 100 g.

For a 40 g portion: 60 × 40 ÷ 100 = 24 g carbohydrate. The 2 g of sugars are already included in those 24 g. The table shows nutrient quantities; it does not predict how much your blood glucose will change.

Three steps when shopping

  1. Compare similar foods on the same basis, usually per 100 g or 100 ml.
  2. Calculate your own portion. The amount you actually eat may differ from the manufacturer's suggested serving.
  3. Read the whole nutrition declaration, including energy. A claim on the front of the pack is not enough.

What to remember

“No added sugar” describes the composition of a food. To work out the carbohydrate in your meal, you need the nutrition declaration and your portion size.
