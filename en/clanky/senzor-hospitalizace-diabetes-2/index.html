Glucose sensors and hospital admissions in type 2 diabetes: what did the study find?

Among people taking insulin several times a day, admissions for acute diabetes complications were less frequent after they started using a sensor. The study used records from routine care.

Published: 23 November 2026

Terms in this article (1)
  • HbA1c: glycated haemoglobin — HbA1c forms when glucose binds to haemoglobin in red blood cells and roughly reflects glucose exposure over the previous 2–3 months.
Educational content only; it does not replace individual advice from a healthcare professional.

Who was studied

The study included 15,413 adults with type 2 diabetes in Andalusia, Spain. All took multiple daily insulin injections and started using a sensor that tracks glucose throughout the day. The researchers compared health records before and after people started using the sensor.

What changed

Admissions for acute diabetes complications fell from about 75 to 28 per 10,000 person-years. One person-year means following one person for one year. Average HbA1c went from 8.09% to 7.65%. The direct before-and-after comparison did not show a clear decrease in cardiovascular admissions.

How to interpret the findings

Participants were not randomly assigned to groups. Other changes in care may have contributed to the findings. The numbers describe the people studied and cannot be directly applied to everyone with type 2 diabetes. If you are considering a sensor, ask your diabetes care team about suitability and how to use it. Do not change your insulin doses based on this article.
