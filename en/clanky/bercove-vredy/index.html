Leg ulcers: finding the cause comes first

A non-healing lower-leg wound is not necessarily a diabetic foot ulcer. Both veins and arteries matter when planning care.

Published: 13 November 2026

Educational content only; it does not replace individual advice from a healthcare professional.

Different wounds, different causes

A leg ulcer is an open wound on the lower leg. Poor venous return is a common cause, but reduced arterial supply or a combination of causes is also possible. Appearance alone, or simply having diabetes, cannot reliably identify the cause.

Why circulation needs checking

A clinician assesses the wound, swelling, surrounding skin and circulation. This may include Doppler assessment and comparing ankle and arm blood pressures. Interpretation can be more complex in diabetes, and further vascular tests may be needed.

Compression needs an assessment

Professionally selected compression can support healing of a venous ulcer. It can be dangerous when arterial supply is inadequate. Do not start tight bandaging or choose a compression stocking strength for an ulcer without a circulation assessment and professional advice.

What wound care involves

Arrange an appointment with primary care or a wound clinic rather than waiting weeks for the wound to disappear. Cleaning, dressings and review intervals depend on the findings. Antibiotics treat infection and are not routine treatment for every ulcer. Follow your team’s advice about movement and positioning, especially if you also have a wound on the foot.

When to seek faster help

Contact a clinician today for new spreading redness, pus, an unpleasant smell, increasing pain or fever. A suddenly cold pale foot, black tissue or severe general deterioration requires immediate emergency assessment; call your local emergency number (112 in the EU) for collapse or confusion.
